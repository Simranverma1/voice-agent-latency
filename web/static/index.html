A voice assistant that answers in 3.4 seconds instead of 9.6

Talking to an AI only feels natural if it answers quickly. This project builds a voice assistant, measures exactly where every millisecond goes between you stopping talking and you hearing a reply, and then removes the waiting, one change at a time.

The problem

In a conversation, people expect a reply within about a second. A typical voice assistant instead runs four slow steps one after another: wait to be sure you've finished talking, turn your speech into text, have a language model write an answer, and turn that answer back into speech. Each step waits for the previous one to finish completely, so the delays stack up. Our first version took a median of 9.6 seconds to start answering.

The goal was not just a faster number, but to measure each step precisely, so every improvement can be explained and attributed to one specific change.

How it works

  1. Your microphoneThe browser cleans up and shrinks the audio to 16 kHz and streams it live.
  2. Speech to textDeepgram transcribes while you talk and decides when you've stopped (300 ms of silence).
  3. Language modelGroq's gpt-oss-20b writes the answer and sends it word by word.
  4. Sentence chunkerAs soon as the first phrase is complete, it's sent on. No waiting for the full answer.
  5. Text to speechDeepgram Aura voices each phrase; requests overlap so there are no gaps.
  6. Your speakerAudio plays back-to-back in the browser while the rest is still being made.

Every arrow above is timestamped on one clock, so the time spent in each step is measured, not estimated.

The biggest win (v2 → v3) needed no faster model and no new vendor. The baseline waited for the entire answer to be written and turned into a complete audio file before playing a single sound, even though you only notice the wait until the first sound. Starting to speak after the first phrase removed most of that wait. It's purely a change in scheduling.

How it's measured

BENCHMARK

20 recorded questions replayed in real time on a laptop in India, calling US-hosted services. The clock starts at the last moment of real sound in the recording and stops when the first reply audio goes to the sound card. Identical audio for every version, so differences come from the pipeline, not the speaker.

LIVE

The live demo runs the final version (v5) on a server, and shows two numbers for every turn: the server's time (your speech end arriving → first reply audio sent) and the browser's time (you stop talking → reply audio starts playing). The gap between them is the internet trip to the server plus audio buffering. Live numbers depend on your connection and are not directly comparable with the benchmark.

Problems I diagnosed along the way

Every one of these was found by reading the per-turn timelines, not by guessing.

Silent gaps mid-sentence

On a slow link, a short phrase finished playing before the next phrase's audio had even started arriving. Fix: request each phrase's audio the moment its text is ready, and play results strictly in order.

Streaming connections dying silently

A dropped live-transcription connection returned empty transcripts forever without raising an error. Fix: rebuild it after an empty result. Even so, the streaming versions completed fewer turns (13/20 and 17/20), a real reliability cost of streaming.

Requests that never time out

A connection that trickles data never trips a per-read timeout. Fix: an overall 20-second deadline per streamed response, plus one retry on temporary network errors.

A speech detector that never heard speech

The detector learns the room's noise level from the first half-second. The recordings started mid-word, so it learned "speech = background" and waited forever. Fix: feed it 600 ms of silence first.

The agent hearing itself

In the browser, the microphone can pick up the agent's own voice and answer it. Fix: the page stops sending microphone audio until the reply has finished playing.

"When did you stop talking?" is a judgement call

Two reasonable loudness thresholds disagreed by 0.3–2.1 seconds on quiet recordings. That uncertainty sits inside every latency number, including the benchmark's.

Honest limitations

  • Every version uses the same two providers (Groq and Deepgram), so this is an architecture comparison, not a vendor comparison.
  • All benchmark turns came from one location in one session; network quality varies by time of day.
  • The recordings are clean: no background noise, accents or crosstalk.
  • Speech synthesis started noticeably slower in the streaming versions (~940–1000 ms vs ~340–480 ms). The cause is not yet isolated.
  • The biggest remaining live delay is waiting for each phrase's full audio before sending it. Streaming within a phrase is the obvious next step.
